Section 2 of 7

Back to the lab: series vs parallel

Series

Bulbs are lined up one right after another. There’s one path, so charge has to go through every bulb to get back to the battery.

Parallel

Bulbs sit on separate branches, like forks in the road. The charge splits up, and each bit of charge goes through only one branch.

Try it

  1. Click a bulb to unscrew it. Click it again to screw it back in. What happens to the other bulbs?
  2. Use + bulb to add a 3rd bulb to each circuit. Watch the brightness.

Notice how easy it was to add a bulb: one more bulb on the loop, or one more rung on the ladder. That’s the point of a circuit diagram. It’s quick to change because it only shows the connections.

Section 3 of 7

Same circuit, different drawings

In the lab, a lot of you drew diagrams shaped like the lampboard. Those were technically correct! But a circuit diagram doesn’t show what the circuit looks like. It only shows what’s connected to what. You can stretch, bend, and move wires around, and as long as the connections stay the same, it’s the same circuit.

The color trick. Turn on Color the connections. Wires that are connected to each other (with nothing in between) get the same color. Red touches the + end of the battery, blue touches the − end, and any wire stuck between two bulbs gets its own color.

Pair 1: messy vs. tidy

Pair 2: messy vs. tidy

Turn the colors on for each pair. Same color pattern means same circuit. The tidy versions are easier to read because you can see the paths instantly.

Drawing tip. Put the battery on the left and draw one big rectangle. For series, put the bulbs right on the rectangle. For parallel, draw the bulbs as rungs of a ladder between the top wire and the bottom wire.

Quick test. If one side of a bulb touches the red wire and the other side touches the blue wire, that bulb is connected straight across the battery. If every bulb does that, the circuit is parallel.

Section 4 of 7

Voltage, current, and energy

You just finished the energy unit, so let’s use it. The battery’s job is to give charges energy, and the bulbs are where that energy gets used.

Voltage (V)

Measured in volts (V)

Voltage tells you how much energy the battery gives each bit of charge. Here’s a helpful way to picture it: a 3 V battery gives each charge 3 “joules” (3 volts) of energy to carry around the circuit.

The quotation marks are on purpose. It’s not exactly joules, but it’s a great way to think about it for now. Scientists call it voltage and measure it in volts, so that’s the word you’ll see on the circuits.

Current (I)

Measured in amps (A)

Current is how much charge passes one spot each second.

Water picture: liters per second coming out of a hose. A fire hydrant has a much bigger flow than a garden hose.

Following the energy

Chemical energy in the battery→carried by the charges→delivered to the bulbs→dissipated as light and heat

The more energy each charge delivers to a bulb (the more volts across it), the brighter that bulb glows.

Watch the wording. Charge is what moves through the wires, and it doesn’t get used up. The same charge that leaves the battery comes back to it. What gets used up is the energy the charge was carrying. Back at the battery, the charge gets “refilled.”

Section 5 of 7

Where does the energy go?

Think of the bulbs as obstacles in an obstacle course, and each charge as a runner that starts with 3 “joules” (3 V) from the battery.

Series: one course, every obstacle

Every charge has to get through every bulb. If it delivered all 3 “joules” (3 V) to the first bulb, it would have nothing left for the next one. So it has to split its energy between the bulbs, and the bulbs split the 3 V. Less voltage per bulb means dimmer bulbs.

Parallel: pick one path

Each charge takes only one branch, so it only hits one obstacle. It can deliver all 3 “joules” (the full 3 V) to that one bulb. That’s why parallel bulbs are brighter.

Turn on Show voltage in each circuit. You’ll see the voltage across each bulb (how much energy each charge delivers to it) and every path a charge can take from the battery and back.

Series

Predict first, then change the circuit to match each row and use the voltage labels to check. Type in the voltage across the bulb.

Why a “different brand”? Some of you saw this in lab with the Christmas lights. Bulbs from different manufacturers aren’t identical, so instead of splitting the 3 V as 1.5 V + 1.5 V, you might get 2 V across one bulb and 1 V across the other. The split changes, but it always adds up to the battery’s 3 V. In energy terms, the charge delivers all of its 3 “joules” before it gets back to the battery to be refilled.

Parallel

Why does each parallel bulb get all 3? Turn on the connection colors above. In parallel, every bulb has one side on the red wire and the other side on the blue wire, so it’s directly touching both ends of the battery. Each bulb gets the full 3 V. In series, bulb 1 touches the + end on one side but bulb 2 on the other, so it only gets part of the 3 V.

Complex circuits: series and parallel together

A complex circuit isn’t a new kind of circuit. It’s just series and parallel combined in one circuit. The trick is to find which bulbs are in series with each other and which are in parallel.

For each circuit, try unscrewing bulbs and turn on Show voltage. Answer the questions below each one. Predict before you test!

Circuit 1

B and C are in parallel with each other, and that pair is in series with A. Every charge goes through A, then picks one of B or C.

Circuit 2

B and C are in series with each other, and that pair is in parallel with A. A charge either goes through A alone, or through B and then C.
