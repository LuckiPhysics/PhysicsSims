The Impulse–Momentum Theorem

About 13 minutes · Due before class Monday 11/16

Momentum can change: a bat hits a ball, a car hits a wall, you catch an egg. Tonight you'll learn what changes it, why a soft landing hurts less, and why bounces change momentum the most.

Notes · Box A

Impulse is a change in momentum

Look at one object: a baseball. When the bat hits it, its velocity changes, so its momentum changes. Did that break conservation of momentum? No. The ball isn't alone; the bat pushed on it.

The change in an object's momentum is called its impulse, written J:

J = Δp = pf − pi

Δ means "final minus initial."

Box A: Impulse (J) is the change in momentum, Δp = pf − pi. Units: kg·m/s, which is the same as N·s.

Quick check: A cart's momentum goes from +3 kg·m/s to +8 kg·m/s. What is its impulse?

Notes · Box B

From F = ma to FΔt = Δp

You already know what changes velocity: a net force. Watch Unit 1 and Unit 2 combine.

Unit 2: F = ma
Unit 1: a = Δv / Δt, so F = m(Δv / Δt)
Multiply both sides by Δt: FΔt = mΔv
And mΔv is Δp, so:
J = FavgΔt = Δp
This is the impulse–momentum theorem. A force acting over a time interval changes momentum.
Flip it around: Fnet = Δp / Δt. Net force is the rate momentum changes. That's Newton's 2nd law written in momentum form.
Box B: Impulse = Favg × Δt = Δp. Impulse points the same direction as the net force.
Notes · Box C

Same Δp, different force

You stop a 0.50 kg ball moving at 10 m/s. No matter how you stop it, Δp is the same: 5.0 kg·m/s. What you control is how long the stop takes.

Box C: For the same Δp, a longer stopping time means a smaller force. That's why airbags, helmets, crumple zones, and "giving" with your hands work. Doubling Δt halves F.
Notes · Box D

Stopping vs. bouncing

A 0.50 kg ball hits a wall at 10 m/s. Toward the wall is +. Compare a ball that stops with one that bounces back at 10 m/s.

Box D: A bounce changes momentum more than a stop, because the object has to stop and reverse. Always do Δp = pf − pi with signs. Don't just subtract the speeds.

The negative Δp means the impulse, and the wall's force, point away from the wall, back toward where the ball came from.

Notes · Box E

Worked example: bat meets ball

A 0.150 kg baseball is pitched at 30.0 m/s and hit straight back at 50.0 m/s. The ball touches the bat for 0.001 s. Find the impulse and the average force on the ball.

Step 1: Pick +. Toward the batter is +, so vi = +30.0 m/s and vf = −50.0 m/s.
Step 2: Momenta. pi = (0.150)(+30.0) = +4.5 kg·m/s. pf = (0.150)(−50.0) = −7.5 kg·m/s.
Step 3: Impulse. J = Δp = pf − pi = −7.5 − 4.5 = −12.0 kg·m/s (N·s).
Step 4: Force. Favg = J / Δt = −12.0 / 0.001 = −12,000 N. That's about 12,000 N toward the pitcher, roughly the weight of a small car, for one millisecond.
