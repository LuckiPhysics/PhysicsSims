Force–Time Graphs

About 12 minutes · Due before class Tuesday 11/17

Real forces aren't constant. A bat's push ramps up and back down in a millisecond. Tonight you'll read impulse straight off a graph, and learn what a momentum–time graph tells you about force.

Notes · Box A

The area is the impulse

A constant 20 N push lasts 3.0 s. On a force–time graph, that's a rectangle.

Impulse is F × Δt = (20 N)(3.0 s) = 60 N·s. That's the height × width of the rectangle, which is its area.

Box A: The area under a force–time graph is the impulse, which equals Δp. This works for any shape, not just rectangles. Area above the axis is +; area below it is −.
Notes · Box B

Triangles, trapezoids, and friends

Tap a shape to see how to find its area.

Box B: Rectangle: F × Δt. Triangle: ½ × base × height. Trapezoid: split it into a rectangle and triangles, then add. Same area means same impulse, even with different shapes.
Notes · Box C

Same area, different peak

Real collisions look like curves. Here a head hits the ground twice with the same Δp: once without a helmet and once with one.

Quick check: Which collision delivers the bigger impulse?

Box C: Equal areas mean equal impulse (equal Δp). The helmet stretches out the time, so the peak force is much smaller, and peak force is what causes injury.
Notes · Box D

The slope of p vs. t is the net force

Remember from ML3: Fnet = Δp / Δt. On a momentum–time graph, Δp / Δt is the slope.

0 to 2 s: p rises from 0 to 6 kg·m/s. Slope = 6 / 2 = +3 N of net force.
2 to 4 s: flat. Slope = 0, so no net force. It's still moving, just not speeding up or slowing down.
4 to 5 s: p drops from 6 to 0 in 1 s. Slope = −6 / 1 = −6 N. That's a bigger force, pointing backward.
Box D: F–t graph: area = Δp. p–t graph: slope = Fnet. A flat p–t graph means zero net force, not zero momentum.
Notes · Box E

Worked example: a push on a cart

The graph shows the net force on a 12 kg cart that starts at rest. Find the impulse and the cart's final speed.

Step 1: Split the shape. Triangle (0–1 s), rectangle (1–3 s), triangle (3–4 s).
Step 2: Areas. ½(1 s)(60 N) = 30 · (2 s)(60 N) = 120 · ½(1 s)(60 N) = 30 N·s
Step 3: Add. J = 30 + 120 + 30 = 180 N·s = Δp
Step 4: Speed. It starts at rest, so Δp = mvf: vf = 180 / 12 = 15 m/s.
