Stored Energy

About 15 minutes · Review for the Energy reassessment

In R2, work moved energy into or out of a system. Some of that energy can be stored: as Ug when the Earth is in the system, or as Us in a stretched or compressed spring. This lesson is about how to calculate both, how to read the graphs, and what changes far from the Earth.

Box A: Ug = mgh is stored in the object–Earth system (the Earth must be in the system).
• h is measured from a reference height you choose. Below it, Ug is negative.
• Changing the reference changes Ug, but not ΔUg = mgΔh. Only changes in Ug matter.
• Here ΔUg = (2.0)(9.8)(1.00) = 19.6 J no matter where h = 0 is.
Notes · Box B

On a ramp, only the height counts

A 15.0 kg block is pushed 10.0 m up a frictionless ramp tilted at 30.0°. What is the change in its Ug?

Box B: Gravity points straight down, so only the vertical change counts: h = d sin θ, so ΔUg = mgd sin θ.
Here h = (10.0 m)(sin 30.0°) = 5.00 m and ΔUg = (15.0)(9.8)(5.00) = 735 J. The path doesn't matter, only the change in height.
Notes · Box C

Springs: slope is k, area is work

To stretch a spring a distance x, you pull with a force F = kx. Drag x and switch springs. The shaded area under the line is the work you do, which is the energy stored in the spring.

F = kx
Us = ½kx² = area

Drag x out to at least 0.15 m. (Next unlocks once you do.)

Try it. On the stiff spring, go from x = 0.10 m to x = 0.20 m. Doubling the stretch makes…

Box C: Hooke's law: the spring pulls back with Fs = −kx (opposite the stretch or squeeze). To stretch it, you pull with F = kx.
• k = slope of the F–x graph, in N/m. A stiffer spring has a steeper line.
• Work to stretch it = area under the F–x graph = ½(x)(kx) = ½kx² = Us.
• Double x → 2× the force but 4× the stored energy.
Notes · Box D

Worked example

A slingshot is pulled back. The graph shows the force needed to stretch its band. Find the band's spring constant k and the work needed to stretch it 0.40 m.

Step 1: Read the graph. It's a straight line through the origin, so the band obeys Hooke's law, F = kx. At x = 0.40 m, F = 120 N.
Step 2: k is the slope.
k = ΔF/Δx = 120 N / 0.40 m = 300 N/m
Step 3: Work is the area. The area under the line is a triangle:
W = ½(base)(height) = ½(0.40 m)(120 N) = 24 J
Check with the formula: ½kx² = ½(300)(0.40)² = 24 J. ✓
Step 4: Does it make sense? If the full 120 N acted over the whole 0.40 m, the work would be 48 J. But the force starts at 0 and builds up, so the average force is only 60 N. Half of 48 J is 24 J.
Step 5: Going further. The slingshot launches a 0.060 kg stone from rest. If all 24 J of stored energy becomes the stone's kinetic energy, how fast does it leave?
Us = ½mvf²
vf = √(2Us/m) = √(2·24/0.060) = 28.3 m/s
Box D: k = slope = 120/0.40 = 300 N/m. W = area = ½(0.40)(120) = 24 J = Us. All 24 J → K of the 0.060 kg stone: vf = 28.3 m/s.

Drag the cart until it leaves the spring (x = 0 or more). Stop at x = −0.05 m on the way: how much energy is still in the spring?

Box E: With no friction, the total energy of the cart–spring system stays constant (1.00 J), a flat line.
• Us = ½kx² is a parabola. K is its mirror image: K = total − Us.
• At half the squeeze (x = −0.05 m), Us is only ¼ of the total (0.25 J), so K = 0.75 J already.
• Once the cart leaves the spring (x ≥ 0), Us = 0 and K = 1.00 J, so v = 2.00 m/s. The cart is fastest from there on.
Notes · Box F

Far from the Earth: Ug = −Gm1m2/r

mgΔh only works near the surface, where g is nearly constant. Far away, gravity gets weaker, so we use a general formula, where r is the distance between the centers:

Ug = −Gm1m2/r
  • Ug = 0 when the objects are infinitely far apart, so Ug is always negative.
  • Moving apart, Ug gets less negative, so ΔUg is positive, just like lifting with mgΔh.

Escape speed is the smallest launch speed that lets a rocket get infinitely far away. Tap through it:

1. At r → ∞, Ug = 0. The least launch speed means the rocket arrives with K = 0 too, so its total energy is 0.
2. Total energy at launch (surface, r = R) equals total energy at infinity:
½mv² − GMm/R = 0
3. The rocket's mass m cancels:
vesc = √(2GM/R)
4. For Earth (M = 5.97 × 1024 kg, R = 6.38 × 106 m): vesc = 1.12 × 104 m/s, about 11 km/s.
Box F: Ug = −Gm1m2/r, zero at r = ∞, always negative, less negative farther out. Escape: ½mv² − GMm/R = 0, so vesc = √(2GM/R). It depends on the planet's M and R, not on the rocket's mass.
