Power and Efficiency

About 15 minutes · Review for the Energy reassessment

R2–R4 were about how much energy moves into or out of a system. This lesson adds how fast it moves (power), how to read power off a K–t graph, and what happens when a machine wastes part of the energy it uses (efficiency).

Notes · Box A

Power is how fast energy moves

A motor lifts a box at constant speed. The work it does goes into Ug: W = mgh. Power is that work divided by the time it takes:

P = W/Δt = ΔE/Δt

unit: 1 watt (W) = 1 J/s

Change the mass, the height, and the time at least once each. (Next unlocks once you do.)

Box A: Power is the rate energy is transferred: P = W/Δt = ΔE/Δt, in watts (1 W = 1 J/s).
• Lifting at constant speed: P = mgh/Δt. Double m or h → double P. Half the time → double P.
• Same work done faster means more power, not more energy.

Try it. Crane A lifts a load of mass 2m up a height h in time t. Crane B lifts mass m up 2h in time 2t. What is PA/PB?

Notes · Box B

Power from force and speed

If a force acts on something that's moving, you can find its power without a time. Tap to build the formula.

P = W/Δt (definition)
P = F∥d/Δt (W = F∥d)
P = F∥(d/Δt) (regroup)
P = F∥v = Fv cosθ (d/Δt = v)

A car's engine puts out a steady 15.0 kW. Slide its speed and watch the driving force.

Box B: P = F∥v = Fv cosθ, where θ is the angle between F and v.
• Average power: P = W/Δt over the whole time. Instantaneous power: P = Fv at one moment, with that moment's speed.
• Same power, slower speed → bigger force (why trucks downshift on hills).

Try it. A 15.0 kW motor drives a cart at a constant 6.0 m/s, with the force along the motion. What force does the motor supply?

Box C: On a K–t graph:
• Slope = ΔK/Δt = rate of net work = power. Tangent line → instantaneous power; line between two points → average power.
• Change in value ΔK = net work (work–energy theorem). Starting from rest, Wnet = the value of the graph.
• Area under a K–t graph means nothing useful in this class.
• Here the slope shrinks as air resistance grows: the ball still speeds up, but more slowly.

Notes · Box D

Efficiency: not all the energy is useful

An electric motor lifts a 20 kg box up 3.0 m. The useful energy out is mgh = (20)(9.8)(3.0) = 588 J. Real motors also heat up, so they need more than 588 J in. Slide the efficiency.

efficiency = useful energy out ÷ energy in

Useful out

Box D: efficiency = Euseful out / Ein (or Pout/Pin), always less than 1 (100%).
• Energy in = useful ÷ efficiency. Divide, so the input is bigger than the useful part.
• The rest isn't destroyed: it becomes thermal energy (and sound).

Try it. A winch is 25% efficient. It must give a load 600 J of useful energy. How much energy does it take in?

Notes · Box E

Worked example: the elevator

A 1000 kg elevator car carries a maximum load of 800 kg. A constant friction force of 4000 N resists its motion. What minimum power must the motor deliver to lift the fully loaded car at a constant 3.0 m/s upward? (Use g = 10 m/s², as on the class slide.)

Step 1: Constant speed means balanced forces. Moving up, friction points down (against the motion), along with the weight. So T = mg + f.
Step 2: Find T.
T = (1800)(10) + 4000 = 22,000 N
Step 3: Use P = Fv. T points the same way as v, so cosθ = 1:
P = Tv = (22,000 N)(3.0 m/s) = 66,000 W = 66 kW
Step 4: Where does it go? Each second the motor supplies 66,000 J: (1800)(10)(3.0) = 54,000 J into Ug, and (4000)(3.0) = 12,000 J lost to friction. K doesn't change.
Going further: moving down at 3.0 m/s. Now friction points up, so T = mg − f = 18,000 − 4000 = 14,000 N, still up. But v is down, so θ = 180°:
P = Tv cos180° = −42,000 W = −42 kW
Negative power means the motor takes energy out: it works as a brake.
Box E: Constant speed → T = mg + f = 22,000 N. P = Tv = 66 kW (54 kW into Ug + 12 kW to friction). Going down: T = 14,000 N, P = −42 kW (motor acts as a brake).

Before you go

Check that Boxes A–F on your notes sheet are filled in, then write the big idea at the bottom in your own words.

  • P = W/Δt = ΔE/Δt, in watts (J/s). Same work in less time → more power.
  • P = Fv cosθ for a force on a moving object. Average power uses the whole time; instantaneous uses the speed right then.
  • K–t graph: slope = power, change in value = net work.
  • Efficiency = useful out ÷ in. To find the input, divide by the efficiency.
